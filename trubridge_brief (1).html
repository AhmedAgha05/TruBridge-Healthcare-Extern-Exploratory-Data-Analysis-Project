Practitioner's Brief: Health Insurance, Housing Insecurity, and Diabetes Prevalence

Summary

My brief studies 3,143 U.S counties that have higher rates of adults aged 18-64 lacking health insurance access and housing insecurity rate are associated with higher rates of diagnosed diabetes. My scatter dot chart shows as the lack of insurance rate increases so does the diabetes prevalence rate for U.S counties also meaning that majority of the U.S counties lack of insurance rate and diabetes prevalence rate have a line trend of a constant rate of increase.

Background

What makes diabetes so awful is that it can cause blood sugar levels to fluctuate, which can lead to many organ failures and raise the risk of heart failure, kidney problems, and even blindness. So, by conducting this research, I will be able to evaluate how a lack of insurance access and housing insecurity influence diabetes prevalence, allowing me to determine whether insurance access is a major issue in all U.S. counties due to the inability to help prevent diabetes early by having insurance and attending doctor visits. This original dataset consists of 229,00 rows of health measures people had across U.S counties but, I filtered it now to certain important measures for my analysis so each county appeared once resulting in me now having 3,143 rows of U.S counties. The dataset was put together via the CDC's Behavior Risk Factor Surveillance System, which is essentially a phone survey.

Methods

I used a test used to show the relationship between two variables, one variable is independent variable also called the predictor for me I have two which is lack of insurance rate and housing insecurity rate and the dependent variable is the outcome variable in relation to diabetes prevalence so I could see how much each factor mattered on its own, while accounting for the other. Resulting in me finding that for housing insecurity, and diabetes prevalence rate, housing insecurity showed more than twice the effect on diabetes prevalence rate compared to lack of insurance rate. My biggest limit is that my two variables explain most of the differences in diabetes rates between counties, there is still 26% of the difference that remains unexplained which concerns me since it's likely driven by a third variable I did not study. This means something I did not measure like income, food stamps, food insecurity, which can also be influencing my variables.

Findings

The analysis showed me that for U.S counties with higher lack of insurance rates mostly have higher diabetes prevalence rates as lack of insurance rate goes up so does diabetes prevalence showing a relationship between the two. There is a lot of clustering for U.S counties for lack of insurance rate from 7.5% to 15% and clear growth in diabetes prevalence. There is also clustering in the number of people with diabetes in U.S. counties, with rates mostly between 5 and 20 percent. Some counties stand out because they have a diabetes prevalence rate between 30 and 45% and a lack of insurance rate between 17.5% and 21%. However, I also saw that the lack of insurance rate in some U.S. counties was between 17.5% and 22.5% and the diabetes prevalence rate was between 10 and 20%. Finally, I also discovered how much of the difference in diabetes prevalence rates between U.S counties is explained by these two factors I picked: lack of insurance access and housing insecurity. The closer this number is to 1 the more it explains. About three quarters of the analysis was made up of my two factors which lines up with the unmeasured factors I mentioned earlier like food insecurity, and food stamps.

Implications

Health teams and administrators might consider prioritizing programs that address both insurance access and housing stability, since housing insecurity showed roughly twice the impact on diabetes rates. Programs offering housing assistance alongside insurance enrollment support could be especially valuable for counties facing both challenges. However, since this analysis cannot rule out that other unmeasured factors like income or food insecurity may also be driving these patterns, further research into these related factors would help confirm where resources are best directed.
